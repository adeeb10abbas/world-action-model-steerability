\begin{abstract}
Success under one instruction can conceal sensitivity to how the same goal is described. We study this problem in three world--action models by varying spatial descriptions while keeping the starting scene, requested placement, and object to move fixed. Across 864 rollouts in four RoboLab scenes, reference-first descriptions produce fewer stable placements, with an average gap of 12.2 percentage points concentrated in particular relations. Execution examples and episode-end checks also reveal that a placement score can pass despite moving the wrong object or losing the arrangement. These analyses identify where performance depends on wording and where success checks overstate instruction following, supporting targeted model testing and better task evaluation.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{World--Action Models, Spatial Language, Robot Evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
World--action models (WAMs) generate robot actions together with predictions of future observations~\citep{kim2026cosmospolicy}. Success under one instruction leaves open whether the policy would succeed under an equivalent description. A robot must identify which object to move and where to place it, even when the relation is described from a different object's perspective.

Prior work evaluates both responsiveness to changed goals and consistency under equivalent instructions. TRI's LBM study tests language conditioning in visually similar scenes~\citep{tri2025lbm}. CAST improves vision--language--action (VLA) instruction following by associating diverse descriptions with atomic commands and synthesizing actions for alternatives to recorded trajectories~\citep{glossop2025cast}. LIBERO-CF tests alternative goals in familiar scenes~\citep{fang2026counterfactual}, while other evaluations examine language perturbations~\citep{fei2026liberoplus}, meaning-preserving paraphrases from shared starts~\citep{kim2026liberopara}, and irrelevant context~\citep{moskalenko2026bring}.

Within WAMs, SG-WAM studies instruction alignment and language perturbations~\citep{he2026sgwam}. We examine spatial reference reversal: ``mustard right of box'' and ``box left of mustard'' describe the same arrangement while both instructions require moving the mustard (Table~\ref{tab:setup}). The same goal may be achieved through different action sequences.

We evaluate three released WAMs in RoboLab~\citep{yang2026robolab}, holding the initial scene, requested placement, and object to move fixed while changing the relational description. The comparison identifies wording-sensitive relations without changing the physical task. We then compare placement scores with episode-end outcomes and selected executions to examine what a reported success represents. This gives model developers concrete cases for testing language consistency and benchmark designers evidence for refining success criteria.

\section{Paired spatial evaluation}
\paragraph{Change the description, hold the goal fixed.}
We use four RoboLab scenes: cube and bowl, butter and raisin box, mustard and raisin box, and two bowls. Goals are left/right/front/behind for the cube, left/right/supported placement on top for butter and mustard, and both bowl-stacking orders. Seven of the twelve goals come from RoboLab; we add five placements using the same scenes and objects.

Table~\ref{tab:setup} defines the three instruction forms. Direct (D) uses RoboLab wording where available. Mover-first (S) and reference-first (I) refer to the object named first after ``so that,'' with the relation reversed in I. All forms explicitly ask to move the same object.

For supported placement, ``on top of and supported by'' becomes ``underneath and supporting.'' For bowls, the latter does not explicitly require the nesting used by the success check. We show those results separately.

\paragraph{Compare models from the same starts.}
We evaluate the DROID versions of Cosmos3 Nano~\citep{nvidia2026nano}, Cosmos3 Edge~\citep{nvidia2026edge}, and FLUX 3 Action~\citep{bfl2026fluxaction}. Nano and FLUX perform strongly on RoboLab's leaderboard; Edge provides a smaller Cosmos-family comparison~\citep{robolab2026leaderboard}. Inputs are one wrist and two exterior cameras plus robot state. After small object-position and orientation changes, we retain the first eight starts per scene solvable by a scripted controller. Every model runs every goal and description from these starts: 864 episodes, with identical initial states and observations across paired runs.

Policies execute 32 actions at a time at 15\,Hz, using the same inference seed and fixed settings for each model. Each episode lasts 20--40\,s, depending on the scene, and continues after the first successful placement. Full instructions and evaluation settings accompany the paper.

\paragraph{Separate reaching a placement from completing the task.}
We count a \emph{stable spatial outcome} when, for at least one second, the requested arrangement holds, the target object has the required support and moves slower than 2\,cm/s, and the gripper is no longer touching it. Spatial directions are defined relative to the robot. This outcome can occur at any point in the episode. An additional analysis checks whether it holds during the final second. Neither score verifies which object was moved: moving the reference may also make the relation true.

For each matched pair, we subtract the reference-first outcome (1 if reached, 0 otherwise) from the mover-first outcome: S$-$I. Positive differences favor mover-first wording. D$-$S tests expanding the direct instruction. We average over goals within each scene, then equally across scenes and models, reporting gaps in percentage points.

Cube-right, butter-right, and mustard-left hold initially and pass under all instructions, contributing zero to the at-any-time differences. Goals requiring a new arrangement are shown separately. Confidence intervals resample starts within scenes, keeping models, goals, and descriptions paired. Sign-flip tests use Holm correction for the two comparisons.

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Overall placement performance.}
Averaging scenes equally, the stable-placement rate is 43.2\% during the episode and 21.8\% at the end (Figure~\ref{fig:goals}). Excluding already-satisfied goals gives 26.8\% and 15.3\%, respectively.

\paragraph{Reference-first descriptions produce fewer stable outcomes.}
Mover-first wording outperforms reference-first by 12.2 percentage points (95\% interval: 8.6--15.4; adjusted $p<0.001$), with the same direction for all three models (Figure~\ref{fig:wording}). D$-$S is smaller and inconclusive. The end-of-episode comparison gives a similar S$-$I gap.

\paragraph{A few relations drive the average.}
Mustard-right has the largest gap, consistently across models. The pooled gap remains positive without bowl stacking. The cube scene has no average S/I difference, while cube-behind favors reference-first wording (Figure~\ref{fig:goals}).

\section{Discussion}
Following controlled simulator evaluation~\citep{li2025simpler}, we compare descriptions from matched starts to keep physical conditions fixed. The per-relation results then identify where further testing would be most informative: a pooled score alone obscures the contrast between mustard-right and the cube scene. These cases provide targets for assessing whether instruction reformulations or training changes improve consistency across descriptions of the same goal.

Execution inspection and episode-end checks clarify what the robot actually accomplished. In Figure~\ref{fig:examples}, Nano passes the spatial check by moving the bowl; roughly half of all episodes with a stable outcome lose it before the end. These findings motivate separate checks for the object moved, the arrangement reached, and its persistence under continued control. LIBERO-CF similarly distinguishes object contact from task success~\citep{fang2026counterfactual}.

\section{Conclusion}
A WAM's ability to complete a task under one description is only part of instruction reliability. Evaluating equivalent descriptions from shared starts exposes wording-sensitive behavior, while execution and retention checks reveal what placement scores miss. Together, these analyses provide concrete targets for improving language consistency and task evaluation.
